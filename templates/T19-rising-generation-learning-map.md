# T19 — Rising-generation learning map

## Purpose

A one-page map of what a family teaches about money and stewardship at each stage of a member's life, who teaches it, and how the practice supports it — plus a per-member learning plan reviewed at the annual family meeting. It turns "we should educate the kids" into named activities with owners and dates.

## How to use

- The LA fills the map with the family during the family discovery (SOP 41) or a family meeting (SOP 43); parents and grandparents own most rows; the practice owns the rows marked *practice*.
- One learning-plan form per member aged roughly 13 and up, or any adult new to financial responsibility (SOP 42). Reviewed annually; the member keeps a copy.
- Nothing on this map is graded. Progress is described, not scored; the point is competence and confidence, not a career in finance.

## Part 1 — The age-staged map

| Stage | Core message | Activities the family runs | Who teaches | Practice support | Milestone |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5–8 | "We have more than enough — and we share." | Money as a physical thing: coins, a purchase counted out, a small gift chosen and given; the first grant in the grandchild philanthropy | Parents; grandparents | None beyond coaching the parents on the "are we rich?" answer (below) | Has handled cash and made a gift |
| 9–12 | Industry and choices | Allowance in three parts (spend / save / give); a saving goal reached; a site visit to a charity before proposing a grant; first look at a simple statement | Parents; grandparents; an aunt or uncle | Sample statement for the family's use | Has proposed and presented a grant |
| 13–18 | Skills and values | A small account in their own name, statements read monthly; the story of how the wealth was made, with the work and risk in it; a real case made for a large purchase (cost, alternatives, reasons); a share of expensive wants funded by the teen | Parents; aunts/uncles for the first specific conversation about the family's wealth | Practice: one session on reading a statement; the "are we rich?" frame coached | Reads a statement; has made and lost an argument for a purchase |
| 18–21 | Managing independence | A budget that lasts; a first mistake with real money treated as a lesson, not a stigma; notice of any trust the member is a beneficiary of, with the mechanics explained; first attendance at a family meeting as a listener | Parents; trustee (introductory meeting, SOP 46) | Practice: member Discovery on the compressed path (SOP 42); notebook set up (T08) | Has attended a family meeting; knows the structures that name them |
| 21–25 | Structures and purposes | Introduced to the entities, trusts, and their purposes; a beneficiary's responsibilities explained; a managed slice to run with coaching; a seat on the investment or grants committee; work outside the family enterprise; possibly a period abroad | Trustee; the family's investment lead; the practice | Practice: investment-plan meeting; managed-slice coaching; committee prep | Has managed money with real consequences for a year |
| 25+ | Interdependence | Full participation in governance; a role in one engagement channel (business, philanthropy, investment committee, property, family-office function, funded venture); mentoring the next stage; a personal mission statement and résumé updated annually (T18) | The council; elders; mentors | Practice: full consultative sequence; learning plan reviewed annually | Holds a role with real responsibility |

## Part 2 — Principles the family agrees (tick the ones adopted)

- [ ] Allowances are for learning, not reward or punishment; never withdrawn as discipline; never topped up when spent — running out is the lesson.
- [ ] Wealth reduces the number of natural money lessons; the family creates them deliberately (cash purchases, price comparisons, a budget for a trip).
- [ ] The wealth story is told with the work, the risks, and the failures in it — not to instil guilt, to explain what success costs.
- [ ] Expensive requests get a hearing and a decision with reasons; "no" is a legitimate, explained answer.
- [ ] A first financial mistake in early adulthood is normal and is met with a plan, not a stigma.
- [ ] Each member works outside the family enterprise before joining it (suggested: three to five years).
- [ ] Competence takes time: roughly a thousand hours of real involvement to be competent with family money; several thousand to be good. Plan for it.
- [ ] Every member may hold a managed slice of family capital to learn on, sized so nothing important is at risk.

## Part 3 — The "are we rich?" frame (for parents)

1. Hear the question as one about security, not the balance sheet. Ask gently what prompted it — never as an interrogation.
2. Do not deny it and do not deflect to richer families; both are discovered later as evasions.
3. Use three categories — *not enough*, *enough*, *more than enough* — place the family in the third, and pair it with what the family does for those who do not have enough.
4. Stop there unless the child asks more. Note the conversation on the member's learning plan.
