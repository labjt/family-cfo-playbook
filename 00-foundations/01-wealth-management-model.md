# 01 — The wealth management model and operating principles

One page that every other document assumes. The model says what the practice delivers; the principles say how the Advisor behaves while delivering it.

## Purpose

- State the service model in a form the whole team can recite.
- Fix the positioning the practice sells, so it never markets one service and delivers another.
- List the operating principles that resolve most judgment calls without a meeting.

## The model

**Wealth management = investment consulting + advanced planning + relationship management.**

| Component | What it means here | Where it lives in the library |
|---|---|---|
| Investment consulting (IC) | Competence in the basics of managing the family's investable assets, sufficient to oversee every other professional's work; delivered through the investment plan and the firm's platform | SOP 12, 13, 24 |
| Advanced planning (AP) = wealth enhancement + wealth transfer + wealth protection + charitable planning | The family's financial house in order beyond the portfolio: taxes on returns; passing assets between generations the way the family intends; protection from creditors, litigants, and catastrophe; giving that serves the family's aims | SOP 30, 31, 32, 46–48 |
| Relationship management (RM) = client relationships + expert-network relationships | Deep, consultative relationships with the family; a managed network of specialists; coordination with the advisors the family already has | SOP 05, 10, 20, 21, 31, 33 |

**Positioning:** the family's **personal chief financial officer** — the one professional who keeps the whole picture in mind, thinks through challenges with the family, and coordinates every specialist, inside [Firm] and outside it, to carry out the decisions.

**Why this model:** advisors who actually practice it serve fewer, larger families more deeply and earn several times what investment generalists earn; the families get one accountable professional instead of a dozen unconnected ones.

!!! extension "Beyond Bowen"
    **Complete wealth.** At $30M and above, the family's wealth is five capitals — human (the members themselves), intellectual (what they know), social (their relationships and their capacity to decide together), spiritual (a shared intention larger than any one member), and financial. Financial capital is the tool that grows the other four. Time is measured in generations: 20, 50, and 100 years. A family flourishes when its individual members flourish inside a culture that supports it. Every recommendation in this library is tested against that, not only against tax and return.

## What "comprehensive" means at this level

- **Entities and trusts, not accounts.** The unit of planning is the family's structure chart: households, operating companies, holding entities, trusts, foundations — and the decision-maker for each.
- **Multiple advisors are the norm.** No family at this level has one advisor; the practice coordinates rather than competes (SOP 33).
- **Governance is part of the plan.** How the family decides together determines whether any structure survives a generation (SOP 45).
- **The rising generation is a client of the plan** even when it is not a client of the practice — someone must advocate for it (SOP 40, 42).
- **Transitions, not transactions.** A sale, a death, a marriage, a succession is a multi-year transition; no single transaction matters unless it improves the transition it sits inside.

## The positioning choice

Two legitimate kinds of professional serve wealthy families. A **personne d'affaires** supplies knowledge and products for the stewardship of financial capital. A **personne de confiance** supplies, in addition, the courage the family needs to act on what it already knows, and works to grow the family's human and intellectual capital. Both are honorable; the failure is to market one and deliver the other. This practice sells the second. Its procedures — the family layer, the advanced-planning loop, the do-no-harm gate — are the proof, and its fee conversation (SOP 14) says so plainly.

## Operating principles

1. **Do no harm.** Before any long-term structure, ask what unintended consequences it could have for the people it is meant to serve — and put the answer to the client (SOP 20, 30).
2. **Eighty percent of the time, do nothing.** Most apparent problems resolve with time and new information. When a client insists on something they would not want done if calm, be tardy: give it the weekend.
3. **Small early joint successes.** Engineer a small decision the family can make together and win at before asking it to make a hard one (SOP 16, 43).
4. **Transitions, not transactions.** Frame every event by the transition it belongs to; rename "succession" to "transition" in the family's vocabulary.
5. **Serve as if this were the only family you will ever serve.** Capacity is protected by the roles and triggers in SOP 03 and 70 so that this can be true.
6. **Never mediate a marriage.** Serve the couple; refer the relationship.
7. **Who pays, who is the client, who advocates for the rising generation.** Answer all three on T05; if the third answer is "no one," act (SOP 40).
8. **The client is always yours to be responsible for.** Borrowed and external experts do the work; the Advisor answers for it.
9. **No surprises.** Anything material is told before the meeting, never in it; the family's central team hears about a recommendation before the family does.
10. **Whoever has the ball is the quarterback.** The person who receives a request owns it to completion.
11. **Say which service you sell.** Knowledge and products, or courage and complete wealth — and deliver the one you named.
12. **Confidentiality is structural.** An adult's information moves only with that adult's written consent (SOP 02).
