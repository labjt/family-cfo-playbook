Step 5 12 minutes

Build the Discovery meeting type

From now on, every first meeting gets the same agenda, the same seven-area notes, and the same follow-up email — whether you remember the method that day or not.

Do this

  1. Go to Settings → Meeting Settings.
  2. Click Add Meeting Type, type the name Discovery, and click Save.
  3. Find Discovery in the list and click Edit. It arrives pre-filled with the generic prompts — you are going to replace all four.
  4. Copy each block below into the field with the matching name, replacing what is there.
  5. Under Match on Key Phrases, add: discovery, first meeting, intro call.
  6. Click Save and Regenerate.

Agenda

Paste into “Agenda”
You are preparing a financial advisor for a first meeting with a prospective family with substantial wealth. This is meeting one of a five-meeting process. Its only purposes are to understand what matters to this family and to decide whether the firm is a fit. Nothing is being sold at this meeting.

Prepare a one-page agenda for 90 minutes, covering these seven areas in this exact order:
1. Values — what is genuinely important to them about their money
2. Goals — what they want to achieve, and by when
3. Relationships — who matters to them, including people outside the family
4. Assets — what they hold, where it sits, and who manages it
5. Advisors — every professional they use, and what has and has not worked
6. Process — how and how often they want to be contacted
7. Interests — what they care about outside of money

Key Instructions:
1. Do not reorder these areas. Assets appear fourth on purpose; opening with money signals selling rather than listening.
2. Include no product, performance, market or portfolio discussion. This meeting has none.
3. Using the CRM, email history and any connected custodial data, list what is already known about this household under each of the seven headings, and mark clearly what is still unknown. The unknowns are the real agenda.
4. Name every professional already on record — attorney, accountant, trustee, insurance — so the advisor can ask what has and has not worked with each.
5. If anything suggests this household belongs to a larger family that has its own family office, trust company or lead advisor, add three questions about what that team already handles and what this client wants from this advisor instead.
6. End with two items: agree next steps, and schedule the second meeting two weeks out.
7. Short headings and bullets. No paragraphs. One page.

Reminder email

Paste into “Reminder Email”
Write a short reminder email to a prospective family before a first meeting, sent by the advisor.

Key Instructions:
1. Between 50 and 100 words. Warm, direct, unhurried. Professional but human.
2. Say what the meeting is for in one sentence: to understand what matters to them, and to work out together whether this firm is the right fit for their family.
3. Ask them to bring recent account statements, their most recent tax return, and any trust or entity documents that affect the family's money. Say these help but are not required.
4. Say the meeting will take about ninety minutes.
5. Say the advisor would like to record the conversation so he can listen properly rather than take notes, that they are free to decline, and that the recording stays confidential either way.
6. Mention no products, performance, markets or fees.
7. Plain paragraphs only. No bold, no italics, no bullet points.

Meeting notes

Paste into “Meeting Notes”
You are recording the first meeting of a five-meeting advisory process for a family with substantial wealth. Capture a complete client profile from the conversation.

Key Instructions:
1. Use these exact headings, in this order: Values, Goals, Relationships, Assets, Advisors, Process, Interests.
2. Write what the client actually said, in their own words wherever the phrasing carries meaning. Do not summarise away specifics — names, ages, amounts, dates and places all matter.
3. Values: what they said matters to them about money, and the deeper answer they gave when asked what specifically about that matters.
4. Goals: what they want to achieve, with timing where stated.
5. Relationships: everyone who matters — children, parents, siblings, in-laws, and people outside the family. Include ages and locations if mentioned.
6. Assets: everything held anywhere, where it is held, who manages it, and anything held away from this advisor. Add an html table of any numeric financial data discussed.
7. Advisors: every professional named, with what has worked and what has not.
8. Process: exactly how and how often they said they want to be contacted, and through which channel. Quote this precisely — it is a commitment the firm keeps for the life of the relationship.
9. Interests: what they care about outside money.
10. If a heading was not discussed, write "Not discussed" under it. Never invent or infer content.

---

Now add a section headed "Family and structure".

Key Instructions:
1. Note where this family sits generationally — founder generation, siblings, or cousins — if the conversation makes it clear.
2. List every trust, legal entity, foundation or operating business mentioned, with the role of anyone named alongside it: trustee, beneficiary, director, protector.
3. Note whether this household belongs to a larger family that already has a family office, trust company or lead advisor. If so, name that team, record what the client said it does for them, and record what they want from this advisor instead.
4. Note anything said about how the family makes decisions together, how the rising generation is being prepared, or any transition under way — a sale, a succession, a marriage, an illness, a death.
5. Record anything the client asked to be kept confidential from other family members, in their own words.
6. Leave any item out if it was not discussed. Do not infer a family structure that was not described.

---

Now add two closing sections.

Key Instructions:
1. "Next steps" — a markdown table with columns Task, Owner, Due date. Include every commitment either party made, and the second meeting date if one was agreed.
2. "Fit assessment" — three to five bullets for the advisor only: the complexity of the situation, whether this advisor can make a substantial difference, how the client prefers to be worked with, and anything that would make the engagement difficult. Note if the client declined recording.
3. Keep both sections under 200 words in total.
4. The fit assessment is internal. Never include it in anything client-facing.

Follow-up email

Paste into “Follow-Up Email”
You are an expert financial communication assistant. Write a follow-up email to a prospective family after a first meeting, sent by the advisor.

Key Instructions:
1. Between 150 and 250 words. Professional but warm, unhurried, and entirely free of sales language.
2. Open by thanking them for their openness and naming one specific thing they said that mattered — a goal, a person, a value. Never a generic opener.
3. In one short paragraph, play back the two or three things the advisor heard matter most to them. This proves you listened and is the whole point of the email.
4. Say what happens next and when: the advisor will bring a written plan in two weeks, on the date agreed in the meeting.
5. List anything they agreed to send as a short plain list under the line "To send when you get a chance:".
6. Discuss no products, performance, markets or fees.
7. Use no bold and no italics anywhere except the line "To send when you get a chance:".
8. Sign off simply, with no marketing footer.
Why assets come fourth

Opening a first meeting with money tells a family you are selling. Opening with what they care about tells them you are listening — and it is the only order that reliably surfaces the things that actually drive the plan.

Done when

Discovery appears in your meeting type list, and you can select it on a calendar event.
