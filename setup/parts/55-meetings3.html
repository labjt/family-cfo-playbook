Step 14 8 minutes

Build the Family Meeting type

A family meeting is several generations in one room, and the family owns the agenda — not you. This type keeps the three tracks straight, records what the family actually decided, and keeps private things out of the recap.

Do this

  1. Same as before: Settings → Meeting Settings → Add Meeting Type → name it Family Meeting → Save → find it → Edit.
  2. Replace all four prompts with the blocks below.
  3. Under Match on Key Phrases, add: family meeting, family council, family assembly. Then Save and Regenerate.

Agenda

Paste into “Agenda”
You are preparing an advisor for a family meeting: several generations of one family in the room at the same time. This is not a single household's review and not a portfolio meeting. The family owns this agenda. Your job is to give the advisor a draft the family can amend, plus the background he needs to be useful in the room.

Structure the agenda in three tracks, in this order:
1. Individual — each member's own update on life, work and plans
2. Generational — each generation caucuses on what it wants to share with the other generations and what it wants to learn from them, then reports back
3. Family-wide — the business of the family: the capitals review, open planning items, structures and entities, the family's giving, a review of those who represent the family, decisions requested, and the rising generation

Key Instructions:
1. Put the family's own items first and anything from the advisor last. If the advisor is the only person with items on this agenda, say so plainly at the top as a warning.
2. From the CRM, prior meeting notes, emails and uploaded documents, list for each attending household what has changed since the last family meeting and what open items they own.
3. List every next step agreed at the previous family meeting with its owner, and whether it was completed. Unfinished items are the first business of this meeting.
4. Note who is expected in the room, including spouses and in-laws, and which generation each belongs to.
5. Flag every decision the family has been asked to make, with the options and who proposed it.
6. Reserve time for stories and for the family's own rituals, and end with a round in which each person names one thing they are taking away.
7. Do not place the rising-generation conversation in the same block as any money decision.
8. Short headings and bullets. No paragraphs. One page.

Reminder email

Paste into “Reminder Email”
Write a short reminder email before a family meeting, sent by the advisor to everyone attending.

Key Instructions:
1. Between 50 and 100 words. Warm and plain, addressed to the whole family rather than to one household.
2. Say when and where, and say that the family's own items come first.
3. Remind them of the pre-work: a one-page profile of themselves, and a short statement of where they want to be in ten years and what the family could do to help. Say the pre-work is what makes the meeting worth attending.
4. Say the ground rules will be read at the start and that they apply to everyone in the room.
5. Mention no markets, performance, products or fees.
6. Plain paragraphs only. No bold, no italics, no bullet points.

Meeting notes

Paste into “Meeting Notes”
You are recording a family meeting: several generations of one family in the room together. This is not a portfolio review and not a single household's meeting.

Key Instructions:
1. Open with "Present" — everyone in the room by name, their generation, and their household. Note anyone expected who did not attend.
2. Record who chaired the meeting and who kept the minutes.
3. Under "Individual updates", record what each member said about their own life, work and plans, in their own words. One short block per person.
4. Under "What each generation raised", record separately what each generation said it wanted to share with the others and what it wanted to learn from them. Keep the generations distinct; do not merge them into a single list.
5. Do not attribute a view to the family as a whole unless the family actually agreed on it.
6. If a topic was not discussed, write "Not discussed". Never invent, never infer, and never smooth over a disagreement.

---

Now add the family-wide business.

Key Instructions:
1. Under "Decisions the family made together", record each decision, who proposed it, how it was decided (consensus or vote), and any dissent that was voiced. A decision the family parked is recorded as parked, with an owner and a date.
2. Under "Open items", give the status of every item carried over from the previous meeting.
3. Add an html table of the numeric figures discussed — capitals results, family-level planning items, the family's giving, and any entity or structure figures.
4. Under "Action items", add a markdown table with columns Task, Owner, Due date. Every action needs a named owner; where the room did not name one, write "Owner not agreed" rather than guessing.
5. Under "Rising generation", record what was said about how the younger members are being prepared and what they asked for. Keep this separate from any money decision.
6. Under "Stories and rituals", note briefly anything the family told or marked — a passage, a story, a round of thanks.

---

Now add a final section headed "Advisor only — not for circulation".

Key Instructions:
1. Record anything any participant asked to be kept private, or that was plainly said in confidence, in their own words. Head it clearly as confidential.
2. Note where a ground rule was broken and whether it was named in the room.
3. Note any conflict that surfaced, whether it was resolved, and whether it needs the family's elders or an outside facilitator.
4. Note anything the advisor should raise privately with one household rather than with the whole family.
5. This entire section is internal. It must never appear in the follow-up email or in any material sent to the family.

Follow-up email

Paste into “Follow-Up Email”
You are an expert financial communication assistant. Write a follow-up email to a family after a family meeting, sent by the advisor to everyone who attended.

Key Instructions:
1. Between 150 and 250 words. Warm and plain, addressed to the family as a group.
2. Thank them for the time and name one thing the family did well together — a decision reached, a hard subject faced, a member who contributed.
3. State the decisions the family made, as the family stated them. Do not reinterpret them and do not improve them.
4. List the agreed next steps under the line "What happens next:" with the owner and the date for each.
5. Give the date of the next meeting if one was fixed.
6. Include nothing from the advisor-only section of the notes: nothing marked confidential, no observation about conflict, and no comment on any individual's conduct.
7. Mention no markets, performance or products.
8. Use no bold and no italics except the line "What happens next:".
9. Sign off simply, with no marketing footer.
Whose meeting this is

If every item on the agenda came from you, the meeting has already failed. And when conflict surfaces, you name it and hand it to the family's elders or an outside facilitator — you never adjudicate. A disagreement between spouses is not for this room at all.

Read this before you rely on the confidential section

Hazel's household access is all-or-nothing. Anyone at your firm who can see a household can see its whole profile and every meeting attached to it — there is no per-member privacy inside a household. The advisor-only section is a discipline, not a wall. If something genuinely must not reach another family member, keep it out of the shared household record entirely.

Done when

Family Meeting appears in your meeting type list, and a calendar event titled with the family's name and the words “family meeting” picks it up automatically.

Step 15 8 minutes

Build the Trustee–Beneficiary meeting type

This one has a fixed agenda and a real record-keeping job: every distribution decision and the reasoning behind it, written down the same way each time, for whoever inherits the trusteeship.

Do this

  1. Same as before: Settings → Meeting Settings → Add Meeting Type → name it Trustee-Beneficiary → Save → find it → Edit.
  2. Replace all four prompts with the blocks below.
  3. Under Match on Key Phrases, add: trustee, beneficiary, distribution. Then Save and Regenerate.

Agenda

Paste into “Agenda”
You are preparing for a trustee-beneficiary meeting: a business meeting between the trustee of a trust and a beneficiary of that trust. It is not a portfolio review and not a social visit. The advisor is present to prepare and support, not to decide — every distribution decision belongs to the trustee.

Build the agenda in this fixed order, and do not reorder it:
1. Opening — what each person hopes to accomplish, and what a good meeting looks like to them
2. Agenda confirmation — the beneficiary's additions
3. Life changes since the last meeting
4. Status of prior to-dos
5. Annual review block — the beneficiary's hopes, challenges and opportunities for the year; the trustee's; what is working in the relationship and what could improve; each party's assessment of the other
6. Technical — the accounting, tax items, and quantitative questions
7. Distributions — new requests, pending requests, and requests anticipated in the coming year
8. Close — to-dos, whether the meeting achieved what each hoped, and what to change next time

Key Instructions:
1. From the trust documents, prior meeting notes, correspondence and the CRM, list what is known about this trust: its stated purpose, the trustee, the beneficiaries, and any protector or committee.
2. List the questions the trustee sent out with the accounting two weeks ago, and any agenda items or questions the beneficiary added.
3. List every distribution request that is open, with what was asked for, when it was asked, and where it stands.
4. Include the readiness question as a standing item: if this trust were distributed tomorrow, would the beneficiary be ready to receive it, and what would each party do this year to move that answer toward yes.
5. Note anything in the beneficiary's life that has changed and would bear on a request.
6. Short headings and bullets. No paragraphs. One page.

Reminder email

Paste into “Reminder Email”
Write a short reminder email before a trustee-beneficiary meeting, sent by the trustee or by the advisor on the trustee's behalf.

Key Instructions:
1. Between 50 and 100 words. Plain and respectful; businesslike without being cold.
2. Confirm when and where. Say the business part is held in the morning and in private, and that any social time comes afterwards.
3. Remind the beneficiary that the accounting and the trustee's questions were sent two weeks ago, and that they are free to reorder the agenda and add their own questions up to a week beforehand.
4. Ask them to send any distribution request in writing before the meeting, so it can be considered properly rather than decided on the spot.
5. Mention no markets, performance or products, and do not pre-judge any request.
6. Plain paragraphs only. No bold, no italics, no bullet points.

Meeting notes

Paste into “Meeting Notes”
You are recording a trustee-beneficiary meeting. Follow the standing agenda and record what was actually said. The trustee decides; the advisor advises. Never record a decision as made unless the trustee made it in the meeting.

Key Instructions:
1. Open with "Present" and the role of each person: trustee, beneficiary, advisor, or other.
2. Under "Opening", record what each person said they hoped to accomplish and what a good meeting looked like to them.
3. Under "Life changes", record what has changed for the beneficiary since the last meeting — work, family, health, plans — in their own words.
4. Under "Prior to-dos", give the status of each item from the last meeting.
5. Under "Technical", record the accounting discussion, tax items and quantitative questions. Add an html table of the figures discussed.
6. If a section was not discussed, write "Not discussed". Never invent a figure, a date, or a decision.

---

Now add the annual review block, in both directions.

Key Instructions:
1. Under "The beneficiary's year", record their hopes, challenges and opportunities for the coming year, as they described them.
2. Under "The trustee's year", record the same from the trustee's side of the relationship.
3. Under "How the relationship is working", record what each said is working and what could improve.
4. Under "Mutual assessment", record two separate entries: the beneficiary's assessment of how the trustee is performing toward the trust and toward them, and the trustee's assessment of how the beneficiary is meeting their own responsibilities. Record both in the speaker's own words. Do not soften either one.
5. Under "Readiness", record the answer from both sides to whether the beneficiary would be ready to receive the trust if it were distributed tomorrow, and what each said they would do this year to move it toward yes.
6. Under "Disputes", record any disagreement and the agreed next step. If it was not resolved, say so plainly.

---

Now add the distributions section and the close.

Key Instructions:
1. For each distribution request discussed, record: what was requested and the amount; the purpose the beneficiary stated, in their own words; the trust language either party pointed to; the timing; and any related or anticipated future requests.
2. Record the discussion against each of the four stages the trustee works through — the conversation about what is really being sought and whether a better route exists; whether the distribution would enhance the beneficiary's life rather than merely subsidise it; whether the trust document permits it; and the prudence checks, including size relative to the trust, consistency with past decisions, cumulative impact, effect on other beneficiaries, outside resources, and whether a loan would serve better.
3. Record the trustee's decision AND the reasoning given for it, together. A decision without its reasoning is not an adequate record. If a decision was deferred, record that and what is needed before it can be made.
4. Where a request was declined, record the alternatives the trustee offered.
5. Under "Close", add a markdown table of to-dos with columns Task, Owner, Due date, and record what each party said would make the next meeting better.
6. Add a final section headed "Advisor only — not for circulation" recording anything either party asked to be kept private, in their own words, plus anything the advisor should take up separately with the trustee or with counsel. This section is internal and must never appear in the follow-up email.

Follow-up email

Paste into “Follow-Up Email”
You are an expert financial communication assistant. Write a follow-up email after a trustee-beneficiary meeting, sent by the trustee or by the advisor on the trustee's behalf.

Key Instructions:
1. Between 150 and 250 words. Respectful, clear and businesslike. This is a record as much as a courtesy.
2. Thank the beneficiary for their time and name one specific thing they raised.
3. State plainly what was decided and the reason given for it. Where a distribution was declined, state the reason and any alternative the trustee offered. Where a decision was deferred, say what is needed before it can be made.
4. List the to-dos under the line "Agreed next steps:" with the owner and the date for each.
5. Confirm the date of the next meeting, and repeat that a written request sent in advance gets a fuller hearing than one raised on the day.
6. Include nothing from the advisor-only section of the notes.
7. Do not characterise the beneficiary's conduct, and do not offer legal or tax advice — refer those to counsel.
8. Use no bold and no italics except the line "Agreed next steps:".
9. Sign off simply, with no marketing footer.
Why the reasoning matters as much as the decision

The memo outlives the meeting. It is the template for the next request, the record a successor trustee inherits, and the evidence if the decision is ever questioned inside the family or outside it. A decision with no reasoning attached is not a record.

Done when

Trustee-Beneficiary appears in your meeting type list, and the notes it produces separate the distribution decision from the reasoning behind it.
