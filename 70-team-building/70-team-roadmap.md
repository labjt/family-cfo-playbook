# 70 — Team roadmap

The practice is a solo profit center inside a federated firm, with specialists borrowed from sister companies. This reference is the map from that starting point to an ensemble: what each stage looks like, the numbers that say when to hire, what the Advisor stops doing at each step, and the principles that keep compensation and ownership sane. Procedures 71–74 execute it.

## Purpose

- Give the Advisor and the firm a shared picture of where the practice is, what the next stage requires, and which trigger fires the next hire.
- Fix the sequence: **borrow first, hire when the numbers say so, delegate in a set order** — so the client experience never depends on who happens to be free.
- Keep compensation and equity decisions principled before there is anyone to negotiate with.

## The stages of a practice

| Stage | Revenue marker (profit center) | What it looks like | Management priority | This practice |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Practice growth | ~$0.5–1M | The owner *is* the business; clients accepted opportunistically; value proposition = the owner's attention | Reach critical mass of relationships so referral sources treat the practice as established | Entry point today |
| Team service | ~$1–3M | Staff hired for named functions (CSA, then SA); delivery becomes a team but the value proposition still rests on the owner; training and management become daily work | Define the service and how it is delivered — decisions made here persist for a decade | Target for the next 24 months |
| Partnership | ~$3–10M | Non-owner professionals serve clients independently; written service standards are followed; explicit client selection; a management team | Institutionalize the reputation — stop being one person's practice | Later |
| Past the owners | > $10M | The value proposition is the firm's, not a person's; departments; ownership and management separate | Combine necessary structure with surviving entrepreneurship | Not in scope |

**The size self-test.** The day there is a client the Advisor has never met, the practice is a true ensemble. The day there is an employee he has never met, it is a large one. The day there is a partner he has never met, it may be too large.

## Ensemble or silo?

The test is economic, not organizational: what share of a principal's income comes from a *shared* bottom line? Roughly 40–50% shared is an ensemble; 5–10% shared is a silo that happens to share an address. Inside a federated firm the same test applies twice — to the Advisor's own future team, and to his profit center's relationship with the firm: if borrowed specialists are paid only per transaction and nothing flows into a shared pool, cooperation will stay transactional (see SOP 71 for the credit rules that keep it from becoming so).

## The borrow-then-build sequence

| Step | Configuration | What the Advisor stops doing | Trigger to move on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 — Now | LA + [Firm shared services] for administration + ISB and EEN per matter (SOP 71) | Nothing yet; but every activity is written down (this library) | Recipes written; capacity strain visible |
| 1 — First hire: CSA | LA + CSA + borrowed specialists | Scheduling, correspondence, paperwork, transfers, notebook, [CRM], reporting assembly | Recurring administrative errors, or the LA spends > 25% of client-facing hours on CSA work |
| 2 — Second hire: Service Advisor | LA + SA + CSA (the canonical delivery unit) | Plan and portfolio production; periodic reviews; routine progress meetings (co-attended → independent) | ~$500–700K revenue **and** ~40 relationships, or the Discovery pipeline exceeds what the LA can prepare for |
| 3 — Third: Associate / Analyst | LA + SA + AA + CSA | Drafting, analysis, packets, tracker upkeep, case briefs | ~$1M revenue with two professionals; relationship size justifies a third level |
| 4 — Depth | A second SA, or an operations lead grown from the CSA seat | Relationship management of a defined book | Revenue per professional back above ~$500K after step 3; a single-point-of-failure risk in either track |

## Triggers — the numbers

| Trigger | Threshold | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Affordability of the first professional | ~$500K revenue. Overhead in a lean profit center runs ~$120–150K (one administrative salary plus space and systems); what is left after tax, minus the founder's *bogey* (the pre-tax income the household needs), is the hiring budget | Compute the bogey on T28; if the surplus covers a Service Advisor's total cost with margin for a market decline, hire |
| Optimal economics | ~$700K revenue with professional compensation (owner's market salary plus the hire) at ~40% of revenue | Treat as the comfortable point; hiring earlier is a deliberate investment, not a mistake |
| Revenue per professional (owner included) | ~$500K is the working norm; add the third professional when two of them pass ~$1M | Review semiannually in SOP 74 |
| Revenue per head (owners and staff) | < ~$200K suggests too many hires too early; > ~$250K says it is time to hire | Review semiannually |
| Relationships per professional | ~40 in a practice serving $30M+ families (more in an affluent practice) | Count relationships, not accounts; a family is one relationship |
| Time per relationship | ~20 hours a year of professional time; ~1,600 client-facing hours per advisor per year | Capacity = hours ÷ hours per relationship; log borrowed and delegated hours on T28 |
| Hire ahead of capacity | A new professional takes up to a year to be fully productive | Start the search when the trigger is *approaching*, not after it is breached |

**The leverage arithmetic.** Delegating half of the professional time in each relationship to a Service Advisor roughly doubles the practice's capacity at a fraction of the owner's cost. With 40 relationships at an average annual fee F, revenue is 40F; if the owner's market salary is S and the SA's total cost is C, professional compensation moves from S ÷ 40F to (S + C) ÷ 80F once the second forty relationships are in place — and the owner's time shifts toward the largest relationships, quality control, and the asks. The arithmetic only works if the SA is productive from the first month, which is why the recipes come first.

## Readiness — five self-tests before the first professional hire

1. Can the role be given specific, consistent responsibilities on day one? Hiring someone to watch the Advisor work is a cost and a demoralizer.
2. Is there a written service process another person can follow? "I listen and respond to what they need" is not a process. This library is the answer to this test.
3. Can the practice offer a career path and tolerate a second professional — including, eventually, giving that person relationships of their own?
4. Will the Advisor hold himself accountable to the process and to the person?
5. Can the practice afford it after the bogey, with room for a bad year?

## The delivery unit

One lead advisor, one service professional, and operations support is the unit that serves a family. It balances capacity, economics, and mentoring, and it remains the building block even inside a large firm. Every SOP in this library names those three roles for that reason.

## The founder's role and the delegation order

| Stage | The Advisor's client-facing time goes to | Delegated in this order |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Everything | — (borrowed specialists take defined deliverables only) |
| 1 | Meetings, plans, asks, coordination | 1. Routine execution → CSA |
| 2 | Discovery, plan presentations, asks, drop/defer/do, network and family facilitation, the largest relationships | 2. Plan and portfolio production, periodic reviews → SA · 3. Routine progress meetings: co-attended, then SA-led with the LA present, then independent |
| 3 | Quality control, largest relationships, business-development handoffs, team management | 4. Relationship management (the client calls the SA first) · 5. Relationship ownership (all meetings including strategy), each gated by the tests in SOP 73 |
| 4 | Strategy, the firm relationship, the next generation of the team | 6. Business development — last; it requires a decade of experience and is outside this library |

Two Bowen habits govern every step: apply drop / defer / delegate / do to each task in that order, and never delegate a step that has not been written down.

## Dashboard and the common-sized P&L

| Ratio | Reads | Cadence |
|---|---|---|
| Earnings before owner compensation (EBOC) | Basic profitability; ~50–60% of revenue is typical for a $1–5M practice, higher for a very small one | Monthly |
| Overhead % of revenue | Spending on "stuff"; ~35% is the guideline | Monthly |
| Revenue per staff head | Staffing pace (the < $200K / > $250K bands above) | Semiannual, in budgeting |
| Revenue per professional | When to hire professionals rather than operations (~$500K) | Semiannual |
| Revenue per relationship | Relationship size and scope; drives minimums and service design | Trend |

The common-sized guideline: revenue 100 → professional compensation (owner at a market salary plus employee professionals) ~40 → gross profit ~60 → overhead ~35 → operating profit ~25. CSA and administrative staff sit in overhead; anyone titled advisor, associate, analyst or planner sits in professional compensation. Vote the owner a market salary for this analysis so that profit is real, not residual.

## Compensation and ownership — principles only

- **Pay the position, not the person's history.** Salary ranges sit roughly ±20% around a credible survey midpoint; movement within the range follows responsibility, experience and relevant credentials, not tenure.
- **Labor and equity are different payments.** An owner earns a market salary for the job done and a return on equity for the capital owned; confusing the two is what makes "equal partners" unequal.
- **Install a compensation system by the second employee professional.** Individual deals stop working past two or three people.
- **Incentives are 5–25% of salary, funded above a revenue threshold** so that a bad year pays zero rather than draining the practice.
- **Partnership, when it comes, follows revenue:** roughly one partner per $750K–1M of revenue; criteria published in advance; equity is bought, never granted — loyalty is expressed in financing terms.
- **Hold an equity conversation once a year even before there is anyone to admit**, so the trajectory is deliberate. Ask of every leader, including the Advisor: who is your successor?

## Failure modes by stage

| Stage | Failure mode | Guard |
|---|---|---|
| Solo | Hiring the first known candidate; hiring before the recipes exist; hiring beyond the bogey; salary-plus-payout hybrids; a veiled partnership promise | SOP 72; this library; T28; SOP 72's offer rules and equity scripts |
| First team | Keeping the new professional out of client meetings; giving them only the small clients; expecting business development from someone a decade short of it; a single junior with no depth; the first hire plateauing and blocking growth | SOP 73; SOP 73's promotion tests; hire ahead of capacity |
| Partnership | Silo economics; agreeing to disagree; cancelled partner meetings; owner salaries anchored to prior-employer income; payouts so high nothing is left to own | SOP 74; the principles above |
