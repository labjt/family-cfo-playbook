# 45 — Family governance readiness

Governance is joint decision-making. Most families need clarity about how a few decisions get made long before they need a system; some never need a system at all. This procedure helps a family decide, without pressure, whether to build governance, what to build first, and what the Advisor runs versus refers to a facilitator.

## Purpose

- Assess whether a family is ready for, and would benefit from, formal governance.
- Sequence the work so principles come before policies and policies before practices.
- Position the Advisor correctly: convener and translator, not the family's government.

## Trigger

Any of: the family discovery (SOP 41) surfaces decisions nobody knows how to make; a transition is approaching (sale of a business, a principal stepping back, death or incapacity, a member coming of age, a branch forming); the family has grown beyond one household's dinner table; a trust or family entity needs a decision body; a principal asks "how do we keep this together?"

## Roles

Owner: LA · Does: AA (readiness notes, drafts of options), CSA (meeting logistics, minutes, record of decisions) · Consulted: elders and any council chair; a family-governance facilitator from the EEN for the first meetings and for any charged decision; counsel for anything that becomes a legal document.

## Timing

| Step | Timing |
|---|---|
| Readiness assessment | Within the family discovery cycle or within 60 days of a trigger |
| Options conversation with principals | +2–4 weeks |
| Foundational retreat | Within a quarter of the family's decision to explore governance |
| Working group | 3–9 months, with updates to the whole family at least quarterly |
| Ratification meeting | When the working group and the principals agree the draft is ready |
| Reviews | Annually at the family meeting; the constitution amended only by its own rule |

## Inputs

- Family executive summary and Family Balance Sheet results (SOP 41, T17, T18).
- Family map, decision rights, and communication map (T14).
- Transitions inventory (SOP 44) and the annual calendar (T15).
- Any existing documents: letters of wishes, shareholder agreements, foundation bylaws, trust preambles.

## Prep checklist

- [ ] The three questions answered on T05: who pays, who is the client, who advocates for the rising generation.
- [ ] The family's current decision-making mapped: who decides what today, and which decisions have caused friction.
- [ ] Member count, generations, and branches counted (the thresholds below depend on them).
- [ ] The LA's own limits stated in writing: what he will run, what he will refer.
- [ ] A facilitator identified in the EEN before the first governance meeting is proposed.

## Procedure

1. **Assess readiness.** Score the family on the signals in the table below. Readiness is *not* size; it is shared intention and a few decisions that need a home.

    | Signal | Ready | Not yet |
    |---|---|---|
    | Shared intention to stay together as a family enterprise | Stated by most adults | Assumed by the founder only |
    | Decisions that need a home | Named (foundation grants, a property, distributions, the business) | "Everything" or "nothing" |
    | Communication | Members already talk about money, however awkwardly | Money is unmentionable |
    | Founder's posture | Willing to move decisions to a group while alive | Wants a structure that enforces his wishes after death |
    | Elders | Someone the family already turns to for disputes and stories | None, or the founder only |
    | Conflict | Present and survivable | Absent (suspicious) or feud-level (refer first) |

2. **Start with the three Ps, in order.** Principles (values, purpose, vision) first — the mission/values session in SOP 43 supplies them. Policies (constitution, trust terms, distribution rules) second. Practices (meetings, executive summaries, education) third. A practice adopted without a policy behind it fades; a policy without agreed principles is contested at the first hard case.
3. **Ask whether a system is needed at all.** Many families need clarity in three or four areas — where the family gathers, how the foundation decides, how discretionary distributions are handled, who speaks for the family to advisors — and nothing more. Offer that as the first option. A system that does not serve individual members' flourishing is make-work.
4. **Set the confederal default.** Each branch or household keeps authority over most matters; only the few matters that are genuinely family-wide go to a family body: oversight of a shared business or foundation, a voice in the direction of shared entities, pooled resources for family learning, relations with outside professionals. Warn against founder-driven centralized designs; they rarely outlive the founder.
5. **Locate the family on the three-stage arc.**

    | Stage | Typical form | Decision rule | Threshold |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | First generation | All adults of sound mind vote; founder holds a veto | Consensus, founder's veto as backstop | Up to one household's children as adults |
    | Sibling generation | Representative democracy of siblings | Consensus sought; equal votes | Until roughly 15–20 adult members |
    | Cousins and beyond | A family republic: branch representatives on a council; branches caucus before sending them | Consensus in the council; supermajority for structural change | From roughly 15–20 members; above ~30 branch representation is essential |

    Design for the *next* stage, not the current one, and build in the amendment rule that lets the family evolve without revolution.

6. **Offer sequencing options.** One well-tested sequence: council → mission and constitution → family bank → investment committee → education program → philanthropy committee, all sitting over the family's long-term trusts. Another: philanthropy first, as the low-risk rehearsal for joint decisions. Choose with the family based on where a decision is already needed.
7. **Hold the foundational retreat (SOP 43).** Questions for the room: why do we want to stay together; what are the strengths and weaknesses of our family culture; how do we want to treat each other; what would a council be responsible for if we had one. Adopt ground rules at this meeting. Use a facilitator.
8. **Form a working group.** Three to five members across generations and branches, one of them from the rising generation. Their job: poll every adult, draft the charter, and keep the family informed. The family executive summary process (SOP 41) is the natural polling tool.
9. **Build the communication plan.** Regular updates from the working group to every member; ideas tested with non-members of the group before they harden; no one surprised at ratification.
10. **Draft the constitution for counsel.** Contents to decide, in the family's words before any lawyer's: statement of purpose; who counts as family (descendants of a named ancestor, spouses, adopted members, partners); values; ground rules for how members treat each other; council charter (purpose, authority — advisory or binding — formation, size, terms, officers, subcommittees, meeting cadence, communication with the wider family); spouse policy (participation, prenuptial expectations, treatment on divorce — set as general policy, never case by case); business-employment policy; conduct-based disqualification from roles and family-bank funds (with treatment funding permitted); dispute resolution; amendment rule (difficult, not impossible).
11. **Define the council's annual duties** so it is a working body: plan the annual family meeting; recommend appointments to trust, foundation, and family-office roles; review budgets of the family enterprise's parts; review the governance system's own performance and report to the assembly; propose amendments; supervise outside advisers; coordinate the annual family balance sheet and income statement (SOP 44).
12. **Design the elders' role deliberately.** Four functions: mediate disputes (equity for all, not a verdict for one); tell the family that it is not following its own rules; keep and tell the stories; hold the rituals. Four qualities: discernment, discretion, discipline, discovery. Elders are nominated by branches, seated by the assembly, serve longer terms, and **cannot act on their own initiative** — they must be asked. Until the family grows its own, a trusted outside adviser may serve as an external elder when asked.

    !!! warning "Caution"
        A governance design with no active role for the senior generation will be sabotaged by that generation. The elder role is where founders go; design it first.

13. **Name a tanist.** Each generation designates an apprenticed successor to the council chair (and a parallel one for the investment committee): no title, no privileges beyond what the council agrees, but the person who learns the family-office matters most deeply, sets the agenda, and keeps discussion on business. Rename any "succession committee" a **transition committee**.
14. **Teach two decision techniques.** *Allies / friends / others* — before any contested decision, each member maps who agrees, who is neutral, who is likely opposed; there is no "enemy" category. *Speaking order* — youngest first, eldest last, so the people who will live longest with the decision are heard before the elders weigh in. Use consensus for structural matters; votes for elections and amendments.
15. **Ratify in the open.** A meeting convened for the purpose, with vigorous debate and an open vote so no one is voiceless. Then celebrate: a story from the family's history, every member signs, a photograph, the signed copy kept by the youngest council member. The first council election usually happens at this meeting; nominees speak to why they want to serve.
16. **Ask every leader the succession question.** "Who is your successor?" — of the council chair, of the operating-company CEO, of the family-office head, of the trustee. Record the answers on the transitions inventory (SOP 44).
17. **Decide what the LA runs and what he refers.** The LA convenes, translates, keeps the calendar, and administers the council's financial duties. A family-governance facilitator (EEN) runs the retreat, the ratification, and any decision where members' interests conflict. Counsel drafts. The LA never mediates between spouses (SOP 40).

## Follow-up

- Within one week of each governance meeting: minutes circulated for comment (CSA); decisions logged; open questions assigned.
- Quarterly during the build: working-group update to the whole family.
- Annually: the assembly reviews the council's performance; the council reviews the system; the mission statement is read at the opening of the annual meeting.

## Do / Don't

**Do**

- Start with the decisions that already need a home.
- Bring critics inside and give them real roles.
- Engineer a small joint decision that succeeds before the hard ones.
- Write the amendment rule before the first amendment is needed.

**Don't**

- Don't propose a system to a family that needs three clear policies.
- Don't let advisers or the founder draft the constitution and hand it down.
- Don't let the council become the place personal and marital matters are litigated; its mission is the family's shared enterprise.
- Don't let "we talk all the time" replace meetings with agendas and minutes.

## Metrics

- Decisions with a named "home" (body, rule, cadence) as a share of decisions the family identified.
- Members participating in the ratification vote as a share of eligible adults.
- Council annual duties completed on the calendar.
