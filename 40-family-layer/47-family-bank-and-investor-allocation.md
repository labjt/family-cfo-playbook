# 47 — Family bank and investor allocation

Two family-level practices sit on top of ordinary asset allocation. **Investor allocation** decides *which family member or entity* holds each investment so that growth accrues where it is taxed least and held longest. The **family bank** lends to family members on terms no commercial lender would offer, because the return the family wants is the borrower's independence and competence as much as the interest. Both need counsel, a policy, and an administrator — the Advisor's role.

## Purpose

- Stop the default in which the oldest, wealthiest, most risk-averse member ends up holding the fastest-growing assets.
- Move growth to the youngest members and the longest-term exempt trusts through documented, arm's-length intrafamily lending.
- Give the family a lending institution that develops borrowers' human and intellectual capital and circulates knowledge across the family.
- Keep the Advisor as designer, administrator, and reporter — never the lender of last resort or the judge of a member's worth.

## Trigger

Any of: a new investment is proposed for the family; the family's mission statement commits to long-term growth of financial capital; a member asks the family for funding (a venture, an education, a home, a career change); trusts with broad investment discretion exist or are being drafted; the family council asks for a lending policy; a member is ready for a managed slice (SOP 42, T19).

## Roles

Owner: LA · Does: AA (growth estimates, allocation memo, loan analysis, register), CSA (applications, minutes, loan register, calendar) · Consulted: counsel and the tax adviser (every loan and every allocation to a trust), trustees, the family council or principals, the family bank's board, [Trust Co.] where trusts lend or borrow, [ESOP Co.] where a member's venture or the family business is involved.

## Timing

| Item | Timing |
|---|---|
| Investor-allocation policy adopted | After the mission/values session (SOP 43) and before the next major investment |
| Allocation decision | For every new investment above the family's stated threshold, before purchase |
| Family bank charter | Within one quarter of the family's decision to lend |
| Loan applications | On the bank's calendar (quarterly), emergencies excepted |
| Borrower reports | Quarterly for investment loans; annually for enhancement loans |
| Bank review and report to the council | Annually at the family meeting |

## Inputs

- The family mission and values statement (SOP 43) with its commitment, if any, to long-term growth.
- The family map and entity/trust map (T14): generations, ages, exempt long-term trusts, trustees and their discretion.
- The investment plan and IPS for each household and trust (SOP 13).
- Counsel's and the tax adviser's guidance on intrafamily loans, gifting capacity, and trust investment authority.
- The family bank charter and forms (T20).

## Prep checklist

- [ ] Every family investment vehicle listed with its owner, horizon, and tax position.
- [ ] Trust instruments checked with counsel for the breadth of investment discretion.
- [ ] Each adult member asked whether they elect to participate in investor allocation.
- [ ] The family's own investment-policy conversation held (step 3), recorded as prompts, not prescriptions.

## Procedure

### A. Investor allocation

1. **Confirm the preconditions.** (a) The mission statement commits to long-term growth of financial capital; (b) each member elects to participate after weighing their own goals; (c) trustees, foundation directors, and managers of family vehicles agree to take part in the same spirit as the overall asset allocation; (d) the trusts grant, or can be given, broad investment discretion — trustees cannot surrender ultimate discretion, so the allocator respects it. If (a) or (b) is missing, stop; run the mission session first.
2. **Name the investor allocator.** A family member, a family-office professional, or the Advisor. The role: for each proposed investment, estimate its long-term growth rate and recommend who should hold it.
3. **Hold the family investment-policy conversation.** Prompts, not prescriptions — the firm's platform builds portfolios: how the family thinks about an operating business as a core holding; whether control of the business is ever for sale; whether illiquidity is a feature the family wants for some capital; where the family concentrates to build and diversifies to protect; how many major investment decisions a year the family wants to make. Record the answers in the family-level IPS (SOP 13).
4. **Map holders to horizons.**

    | Holder | Horizon | Takes |
    |---|---|---|
    | Oldest generation | ~20 years | Lowest-growth, income-producing assets; current cash flow they actually want |
    | Middle generation | ~50 years | Intermediate growth |
    | Youngest members and the longest-term exempt trusts | ~100 years | Highest-growth, highest-risk assets |

5. **Solve the funding problem with loans, not gifts.** The youngest members and exempt trusts usually have the least money; gifts are capped. The primary mechanism is a loan from the oldest generation to the younger members (and, with careful legal advice, to exempt long-term trusts) to buy the growth asset. Rules: arm's-length terms; documented; counsel and the tax adviser confirm the loan will not be recharacterized as a gift; collateral and repayment liquidity planned in advance; the lender's own portfolio analyzed for how much should go into the program. The senior generation receives the interest income it wanted while the growth of that slice of its estate is capped.

    !!! warning "Caution"
        Nothing in this section is tax or legal advice. Every allocation to a trust and every intrafamily loan is designed with counsel and the tax adviser before it is executed.

6. **Decide and document each allocation.** The allocator's memo: the investment, its estimated growth, the recommended holder, the funding route, and the trustee's or member's decision. File with the investment plan; report annually to the council.
7. **Coordinate the trustees.** Trustees hold the family's longest-horizon capital; their investment programs must be aligned with the family's allocation for the program to work. Where the family has a private trust company or common trust funds, every member can participate in every sector regardless of individual wealth.

### B. The family bank

8. **Write the bank's mission statement (T20).** Why it exists — a deliberately high-risk, low-interest lender whose return is measured in members' independence and the family's growth in human, intellectual, social, and spiritual capital — with a values section that incorporates the family's mission statement. Every participant, lender or borrower, concurs in writing.
9. **Keep it informal but ruled.** Not a chartered institution (privacy; freedom to evolve), but with formal meeting rules, officers, a board (with advisers as needed), and written procedures for receiving and deciding applications. Rules vary with who funds the loans — members, trusts, or both.
10. **Define the two loan types.**
    - *Investment loans* — to grow financial and intellectual capital by backing a member's business or an investor-allocation purchase. Process: the borrower prepares a business plan and an application equal to what a commercial lender would require; discusses feasibility with the board and its advisers; once granted, files proper business reports; repays.
    - *Enhancement loans* — to grow human, intellectual, social, and spiritual capital by increasing a member's independence (education, a career change, a start in life). The application states how the loan will increase the borrower's independence and add to the family's capitals; the borrower must be able to explain that to peers and advisers on the board. Repayment is measured in independence and capital as much as money.
11. **Set the terms as policy choices, in advance.** Options the family may adopt: below-market interest; interest-free but repayable in full; grants for defined purposes; a no-bailout rule (the bank funds starts, education, homes, health, ventures — never the consequences of a member's own imprudence); collateral or none. Some families also use the bank to replace commercial mortgages, car loans, and certain insurance, lowering the family's total cost of living. Record the choices in the charter.
12. **Apply the transparency rule.** Every participating member receives every loan application's qualitative portions — purpose, how it enhances, what it adds to the family; personal financial detail may be redacted. This circulation of intellectual capital is the bank's real return.
13. **Include the trusts.** Trusts are potential lenders and borrowers; trustees must understand and agree to participate, and their instruments must permit it — counsel confirms.
14. **Decide and record.** The board meets on the calendar; hears the applicant; decides; records the decision, terms, and reporting duties (T20); the CSA maintains the loan register; the AA reviews reports.
15. **Add the managed slice.** Where a member is ready (T19), the bank or a household allocates a small sum — roughly half to one percent of family financial capital, or a fixed amount — for the member to manage with coaching. Mistakes are the curriculum; size it so they are affordable.
16. **Report annually.** To the council or principals at the family meeting: loans made, repaid, outstanding; what borrowers reported learning; allocations made and their outcomes; policy changes proposed.

## Follow-up

- Within one week of a board meeting: decisions recorded; loan documents to counsel; register updated; applicants informed with reasons.
- Quarterly: investment-loan reports received and reviewed; allocation memos filed with the investment plan.
- Annually: bank and allocation report to the family meeting; charter and policies reviewed; every participant's concurrence renewed.

## Do / Don't

**Do**

- Treat the application process as the education — a real business plan, a real hearing.
- Circulate every application's qualitative portions to everyone who participates.
- Get counsel's and the tax adviser's sign-off before any loan or allocation to a trust.
- Start with one small loan that succeeds.

**Don't**

- Don't let the bank become a remittance channel; dependence on distributions is the liability the bank exists to prevent.
- Don't let the Advisor or the board judge a member's worth; judge the application.
- Don't allocate high-growth assets to trusts whose trustees have not agreed and whose instruments do not permit it.
- Don't present Bonner's investment posture as the firm's advice; it is a conversation menu.

## Metrics

- Share of new family investments with an allocation memo before purchase.
- Loans outstanding, repaid on schedule, and borrowers' reports filed on time.
- Members holding a managed slice or a bank role as a share of adult members.
