# 46 — Trustee–beneficiary relationship

A trust is a relationship among a creator, a trustee, and a beneficiary that happens to live inside a legal document. Most families the practice serves hold the majority of their financial capital in trust by the third generation, and most beneficiaries experience those trusts as a burden. The Advisor sits inside the trustscape — next to the trustee (often [Trust Co.]), the beneficiary, and the creator — and this procedure defines what he does there: help the parties see the relationship, prepare each side for the other, run a real meeting, and turn the distributive function from an afterthought into the heart of the trust.

## Purpose

- Make every trust the practice touches a human relationship first and a legal one second.
- Prepare beneficiaries to receive well and trustees to serve well, so that if the trust ended tomorrow the beneficiary could steward what it holds.
- Give the distribution process a written, transparent, repeatable form.
- Keep the Advisor's role clear: he advises, prepares, and coordinates; the trustee decides.

## Trigger

Any of: a client is or becomes a beneficiary or trustee; a new trust is being drafted; a beneficiary reaches an age of notice or a trust becomes distributive; a distribution request is coming; a trustee–beneficiary meeting is due (at least annually); friction between trustee and beneficiary; a beneficiary's marriage or engagement; a trustee's succession.

## Roles

Owner: LA · Does: AA (trustscape map, meeting packs T22, request analysis support T23), CSA (scheduling, letters, records) · Consulted: the trustee ([Trust Co.] or individual), the trust protector if any, counsel for every drafting item, a counselor or family-governance facilitator from the EEN where relationships are strained, the ISB for trust administration questions.

## Timing

| Item | Timing |
|---|---|
| Trustscape drawn and narratives audited | At Discovery for any beneficiary or trustee client; refreshed annually |
| New-beneficiary introductory letters | Within 30 days of the beneficiary's notice; first meeting within 60 days |
| Trustee–beneficiary meeting | At least annually; quarterly where distributions are active |
| Accounting and trustee's questions to the beneficiary | Two weeks before the meeting |
| Beneficiary's agenda additions and questions | One week before the meeting |
| Follow-up call after a first or heavy meeting | One week after |
| Distribution requests | On the agreed calendar (quarterly for routine, annually for significant); emergencies as they arise |
| Relationship preamble and letters of wishes | Reviewed every ten years and at any material change |
| Protector check-in | Annually, with each party separately |

## Inputs

- The trust instruments and any statements of purpose, letters of wishes, or legacy letters; the trust's investment policy; the last accounting.
- The beneficiary's Total Client Profile (SOP 11) and, in Mode A, the family map and confidentiality matrix (T14).
- The trustee's policies and procedures for distributions.
- The consent on T05 governing what may be shared with the trustee and the family.

## Prep checklist

- [ ] Trustscape drawn for each significant trust: every party, every adviser, the lines between them (see step 1).
- [ ] Each party's narrative about the trust noted — positive and negative assumptions, and where they came from.
- [ ] The trustee's three functions mapped (administration, investment, distribution) and who actually performs each.
- [ ] The beneficiary's current literacy in each function noted in words.
- [ ] Any pending distribution request identified and the request form (T23) started.
- [ ] Conflicts of interest across the trustscape listed, disclosed, and a management note recorded (SOP 48).

## Procedure

### A. See the relationship

1. **Draw the trustscape.** With the client, lay out the people and institutions around the trust as objects on a page — the client, family members, the trust itself, trustee(s), protector, committees, each party's advisers — placed by closeness and connected by lines. Photograph it. Then draw the trustscape the client would *like* to have. The gap between the two is the agenda.
2. **Audit the narratives.** Ask each party, separately, what they believe about trusts in general, about this trust, about the other party, and where those beliefs came from. Ask which beliefs serve them and which do not. Do not argue with a narrative; name it, and ask what one small step would test it.

    !!! script "Say something like"
        "Everyone arrives at a trust with a story about it. Before we look at the document, tell me the story you've been carrying."

3. **Ask the two destination questions** of whoever is in the room: what is the single most important reason this trust exists, and what is the most important outcome you want from it. If nobody can answer, the trust needs a statement of purpose (section C).

### B. Prepare the trustee

4. **Brief prospective trustees at drafting time.** When a client is about to name a trustee — or is asked to serve — walk the candidate through our selection topics (T24, Part 3): first reactions; document homework; self-inventory; yellow flags (strangers as beneficiaries, litigation history, multiple marriages or branches, addiction, special assets, a family business in the trust); and the deeper questions about oversight, disagreement, compensation, liability, and exit. A candidate who arrives already asking these questions is a good sign.
5. **Run the "which cap is on top" conversation** for any family-member trustee: list every relationship the candidate has with the beneficiary (parent, aunt, business partner, friend); the trustee role must outrank all of them whenever the candidate is acting as trustee. Ask the candidate whether they can set the other caps aside; ask the creator whether they believe it.
6. **Position [Trust Co.] correctly.** Where the firm's trust company is trustee, its strengths are administration, investment process, and continuity; its weakness is knowing the beneficiary. Compensate with a Distribution Committee and an Office of the Beneficiary (SOP 48) and with the Advisor's own relationship with the beneficiary.

### C. Give the trust a voice

7. **Encourage the creator to write purpose language for counsel.** A short opening statement of what the trust is for, in the creator's own words — a gift meant to enhance the beneficiaries' lives, not to control them — placed before the mechanics. Counsel drafts; the creator's words lead.
8. **Offer the three letters of wishes.** Creator to beneficiaries (why, and hopes); creator to trustees (how to approach discretion, non-binding); and, later, trustee to successor trustees and beneficiaries (what this trust has meant in practice). Offer a legacy-letter outline where the creator wants to say more (purpose, exhibits, an ethical will, messages to each beneficiary, guidance to trustees, distribution principles).
9. **Broaden the purpose vocabulary** beyond the customary health, education, maintenance, and support: productivity (a start-up, a lower-paid calling, a period of volunteering, a career coach), education defined to include lifelong learning, family unity (gatherings, facilitators, weddings, adoption), legacy (giving, memberships). Counsel decides what the document can carry; the family decides what it means.
10. **Name the trust deliberately.** Five minutes at signing spares decades of statements headed by a surname and a legal phrase.
11. **Write a relationship preamble for existing trusts.** Where the creator is gone or silent, trustee and beneficiary write their own short statement of how they will work together and what they understand the trust's purposes to be. Revisit it every ten years and at any material change: a new trustee or beneficiary, a sale of a key asset, a new life stage, a health change.

### D. Onboard a new beneficiary

12. **Confirm the advance-notice plan (SOP 42).** The parents or creator tell the beneficiary that the trust exists and what it is for; the trustee and the Advisor explain how it works. No beneficiary learns of a trust from a statement.
13. **Exchange introductory letters (T22).** Trustee to beneficiary: who they are, when and where, hopes for the relationship, what will be covered, an invitation to add agenda items and bring written questions, and a request that the beneficiary read the instrument first. Beneficiary to trustee: who they are and what is going on in their life, what they hope for, their agenda items, advance notice of any request, a request for the agenda and materials. The Advisor drafts or coaches both sides where asked.
14. **Design the first meeting** so that anxieties come before nuts and bolts: introductions; the trustee's short account of the role and the journey ahead; the beneficiary's questions and what they think they already know; then, and only then, the mechanics — roles, how to request funds and how requests are analyzed, a sample statement walked through, investments at whatever depth the beneficiary wants. Schedule the one-week follow-up call before leaving the room.

### E. Run the meeting

15. **Prepare the mindset.** Before each meeting the trustee (and the Advisor) decide whether this is a situation for caution or for advancement, and shift accordingly; recall meetings that helped someone grow; plan how to respond to the beneficiary's good news with genuine interest and open questions rather than a pivot to the statements; list the beneficiary's strengths, not their problems.
16. **Use the two premeeting checklists (T22).** Trustee: instrument, correspondence, prior notes; the meeting's highest purpose and what an excellent outcome sounds like; own narratives; any pending request and the question that would open a deeper conversation; the big questions about the beneficiary's self-awareness, calling, mentor, and civic life — and whether the trustee is equipped to help. Beneficiary: the same documents; the running master list of questions; agenda additions; how a request would enhance their life; own narratives; own strengths.
17. **Run the redesigned annual format.** Send the accounting two weeks ahead with a proposed agenda and the trustee's specific questions; the beneficiary reorders the agenda and adds their own questions a week ahead. Meet in the morning, in private (never a restaurant), for the business; keep any social time for afterward.
18. **Follow the standing agenda** (T22): opening — what each hopes to accomplish and what success looks like; agenda confirmation; life changes since last time; status of prior to-dos → **annual review block** — the beneficiary's hopes, challenges, and opportunities for the year; the trustee's; what is working and what could improve; the beneficiary's assessment of the trustee's performance and the trustee's of the beneficiary's; disputes and next steps → technical block — statement, tax, quantitative items → distributions — new, pending, anticipated → close — to-dos, did we achieve what we hoped, what to change next time.
19. **Ask the distribution-readiness question** at least annually, in both directions: if this trust had to be distributed tomorrow, would the beneficiary be ready to receive it — and what would each party do this year to make the answer "yes"?

### F. Handle distribution requests

20. **Set the calendar.** Routine requests quarterly, significant ones annually, emergencies as they arise; the calendar is stated at onboarding and on T23.
21. **Receive the request in writing (T23):** purpose; amount; the trust language the beneficiary believes permits it; timing; related follow-on requests; other requests anticipated in the coming year. Supporting documents as appropriate — invoices, a budget, a business plan scaled to the ask, a statement of outside resources. Where apt, a short essay on how approval would enhance the beneficiary's life. In practice much of this is assembled together in conversation; the form is a thinking aid, not a hurdle.
22. **Stage 1 — the conversation.** Before or after the written request: what motivates it and whether there is a larger purpose underneath; how it squares with the creator's intent and with the document; whether there is a better route than trust funds; whether the beneficiary will have their own resources in it; the best outcome if approved, short and long term; what changes if declined, and what alternatives would exist. Insert a growth question even in routine budget reviews — what does having the trust pay for this cost the beneficiary in independence?
23. **Stage 2 — the enhancement standard.** Is making, or not making, this distribution more likely to enhance the beneficiary's life — meaning their maturation and pursuit of their own aims — or merely to subsidize it? This question will not be in the document; it is the practical expression of the trustee's fidelity to the purpose. It is life-stage dependent: a request from a professional in her forties is not a request from a twenty-one-year-old.
24. **Stage 3 — the document.** Would approval be a reasonable exercise of discretion under the trust's language? If the language is narrower than the purpose, note it for the structural review (SOP 48).
25. **Stage 4 — prudence checks.** Size relative to the trust; the beneficiary's request history and consistency with past decisions; one-off or part of a plan, and cumulative impact; requests anticipated this year and beyond; effect on other beneficiaries; whether outside resources must be considered; whether a loan, or part-loan part-distribution, would serve better (a residence financed as a mortgage keeps the beneficiary's own stake in it).
26. **Communicate the decision and the reasoning.** Transparently, with questions answered. If declined, offer time to brainstorm other routes to the underlying goal. The trustee decides; the Advisor and any Distribution Committee advise.
27. **Write the decision memo (T23).** Process, analysis, decision, alternatives offered, follow-up. Three uses: a template for future requests, a record for successor trustees, and evidence if the decision is questioned in the family or in court.

### G. Develop the beneficiary

28. **Teach the beneficiary's responsibilities**, in our words: understand each trust and its purpose; know what trustees must do; understand the balance between preserving purchasing power and paying a reasonable distribution; grasp asset allocation at a working level; look for evidence the trustee serves all beneficiaries; meet the trustee annually and give an assessment; know how the family's trust structure fits together; read a fiduciary accounting; take up the education offered; know what trustees and professionals are paid.
29. **Build literacy in the three functions** — administration, investment, distribution — to the point that the beneficiary could receive the assets. Use the practice's own reporting and IPS as teaching material.
30. **Offer the Office of the Beneficiary (SOP 48).** A small group the beneficiary convenes and pays for — an accountant who can teach, an investment expert, later a mentor for the psychological and career side — which the trustee acknowledges and supplies with documents. It may draw on family-office and [Firm] resources; it must not be housed in the family office, or it stops being the beneficiary's own. It is transitory by design.
31. **Practice gratitude deliberately.** Once or twice a year, the beneficiary writes to the creator (living or not), or reflects on what the trust has taught them, and where the relationship was difficult, simply on what has been learned. A gift is incomplete until it is acknowledged.
32. **Support the trustee as mentor.** The relationship begins as an arranged one; the trustee's early role is mentor, evolving toward representative as the beneficiary participates fully. When the beneficiary asks why they were given this gift and whether they are worthy of it, the answer must be truthful.

### H. Guard the relationship

33. **Protector check-in.** Where a protector exists (SOP 48), they ask each party separately, once a year, how it is going. In conflict, they ask each party what they themselves did or did not do that contributed, before deciding whether to switch on any power.
34. **Addiction and behavioral provisions.** Where the family has any history of addiction or serious mental-health disorder, raise with counsel — before a problem exists — whether the trust carries workable provisions: discretion to withhold, authority to fund evaluation and treatment, a requirement of compliance and releases, testing, a defined recovery period, distributions to dependents while the beneficiary's are suspended, and an updating mechanism. Principles only; counsel drafts.
35. **Marriage.** On any engagement, run the seven-step prenuptial communication (SOP 22): the family establishes what the member actually brings to the marriage as opposed to what is in trust; each parent clarifies their own feelings; the parents decide whether to leave the decision to the member; the member and the fiancé(e) clarify their own; parents and member talk for understanding; the couple talk; then decide how the process can support the new marriage. Trust interests are disclosed — disclosure is not access — and framed as family history, not protection against a person. Offer the Advisor or a counselor as translator between the family and both sets of lawyers.
36. **When to bring the expert into the room.** For any complex solution — a decanting, a family limited partnership, a distribution committee — the relevant expert joins the beneficiary meeting in person or by phone.

!!! extension "Beyond Bowen — a contrasting drafting menu"
    Some families prefer trusts with no age-triggered distributions, eligibility conditioned on productive behavior, a short list of permitted purposes (career start, education, ventures, arts or humanitarian work, emergencies) and reciprocal duties (serve on committees, help manage property, help protect the enterprise). This is a legitimate design for counsel to consider, but it sits in tension with the beneficiary-agency model above — it substitutes rules for the trustee's discernment and the beneficiary's growth. Say which the family is choosing and why.

## Follow-up

- Within 24 hours of any meeting: recap to the parties as consent allows; to-dos assigned; T22 pack filed.
- Within one week: follow-up call after a first or heavy meeting.
- Within two weeks of a distribution decision: decision memo filed; beneficiary's notebook updated (T08).
- Annually: the trustscape, narratives, and mutual assessments refreshed; the distribution-readiness question asked; letters of wishes reviewed against life changes.

## Do / Don't

**Do**

- Start the analysis of a request at the start of the relationship, not when the request arrives.
- Put the beneficiary's questions before the statements.
- Treat a beneficiary in their sixties as an adult; "newly notified" is not "young."
- Document every discretionary decision.

**Don't**

- Don't let the Advisor, a committee, or a protector make the distribution decision; the trustee decides.
- Don't let a trustee's letter be the beneficiary's first notice of a trust.
- Don't equate a beneficiary with their problems; list their strengths.
- Don't sell perpetuity as a tax product (SOP 48).

## Mode A / Mode B

### Mode A — whole family

- The Advisor coordinates the whole trustscape: trustee ([Trust Co.] or individual), protector, committees, and the family's counsel; the trust map is part of the family profile (T14).
- Where a family member is trustee for a sibling, the "which cap is on top" conversation is repeated annually.

### Mode B — individual within a larger team

- The Advisor supports his individual as a beneficiary within the family's trustscape: prepares them for meetings, helps them draft requests, and attends when invited; he does not redesign the family's trusts.
- Requests and recaps go to the FCT or trustee only as the individual's consent on T05 allows.
- Where [Trust Co.] is the family's trustee and also the Advisor's sister company, the confidentiality wall applies and is stated in writing.

## Metrics

- Trustee–beneficiary meetings held per beneficiary per year (target ≥1; quarterly where distributive).
- Distribution requests with a written request and a decision memo (target 100%).
- Beneficiaries with a documented "ready to receive" plan (target 100% of adult beneficiaries).
