# 48 — Trust system design and review

The ongoing trustee–beneficiary relationship (SOP 46) is continuous; the family's trust *system* changes at events — a new trust, a trustee's death, a beneficiary coming of age, a decanting, a termination, the decision to form a private trust company. This procedure handles those events: designing the roles around a trust so the trustee's highest duty can actually be met, reviewing the whole system on a ten-year horizon, commissioning a Family Trust Review when a transition calls for one, and closing trusts well.

## Purpose

- Design trust structures whose roles — trustee, Distribution Committee, Office of the Beneficiary, protector — are oriented to growing capable beneficiaries.
- Review the family's trust system periodically for fit, cost, succession, and looming problems.
- Handle modifications, successions, and terminations with every stakeholder involved.
- Keep the Advisor as coordinator and translator; counsel drafts, the trustee decides, the family owns.

## Trigger

| Trigger | Response in this procedure |
|---|---|
| A new trust or gift is being planned | Section A (design) and the perpetual-trust counterweight (step 12) |
| A generational transition; a trustee or family leader stepping back | Section B (ten-year review) and Section C (Family Trust Review) |
| A trust will terminate or make a large principal distribution within ten years | Section C, then Section E (termination) |
| The family is considering decanting, reformation, or a private trust company | Sections C and D |
| A trustee dies, resigns, or is to be replaced | Section D (succession) |
| Beneficiaries and trustee are in persistent conflict | Section D (protector) and SOP 46 |
| Every three to five years, regardless | Peer review (step 20); ten-year review refreshed |

## Procedure

### A. Design the roles around a new trust

1. **Start with the creator's exercise, not the document.** Before any drafting, ask the creator to spend several days on one question: who can be counted on to advocate for this trust's highest purpose — the beneficiary's growth and eventual freedom — especially when the beneficiary is not in the room. Write the names down. Then the twist: those people may be better placed as protector or committee members than as trustee.
2. **Choose the protector first.** The person with judgment and practical wisdom from step 1 becomes the protector — a narrow, largely dormant role: get to know trustee and beneficiary; ask each separately once a year how it is going; act only when asked; in conflict, ask each party what they contributed, then mediate; at impasse, remove and replace the trustee, or leave things as they are. The protector never decides a distribution. Because the role is not fiduciary in most places, people who would refuse trusteeship may accept it. Counsel confirms the design.
3. **Use an institutional trustee for administration and process.** [Trust Co.] or another institution does what it does best — administration, investment process, continuity — and is informed on distributions by a Distribution Committee and on the beneficiary's development by an Office of the Beneficiary.
4. **Design the Distribution Committee (DC) for counsel.** Its sole job: get to know each beneficiary before any request, understand the context when one is brewing, and advise the trustee. It never approves or declines. Drafting points: authorize it in the instrument, tied to the purpose statement; costs paid by the trust; members appointed by the creator, trustee, or an independent party in consultation with the primary beneficiaries; beneficiaries cannot add or remove members; five-year terms, two-term cap, age-out; each member nominates a successor; formal review of the DC every three years by trustee, protector, and beneficiaries. Members are chosen for the wisdom to ask broadening questions, the skill to listen before responding, and self-awareness — not technical trust knowledge. Where an existing trust lacks a DC, the trustee may convene one on its own initiative, or counsel may add it by settlement, reformation, decanting, or protector action. **Do not combine the DC and the protector.**
5. **Offer the Office of the Beneficiary (OTB).** The beneficiary's own, transitory, beneficiary-funded team for becoming ready to receive: an accountant who teaches, an investment expert, later a mentor for the personal side. The trustee acknowledges it and supplies documents. It may draw on family-office and [Firm] resources; it must not be housed in the family office. Escalation logic: if a trustee consistently ignores DC advice, that is the moment to switch on the protector.
6. **Size the model.** The full model suits trusts above roughly $10M; below that, a DC of one and an informal OTB. As family wealth approaches $100M, consider whether a private trust company should house these functions (step 22).
7. **Sequence the drafting agenda for counsel** (this is a menu, not advice): purpose language and letters of wishes (SOP 46); fund growth assets into long-term trusts *before* they appreciate; leveraged sales to trusts, grantor-trust techniques, valuation discounts, trust situs and state tax — each as an item counsel evaluates; insurance-funded plans tested against a devil's-advocate list (compounding costs, counterparty lock-in, the fate of a lump-sum payout in the hands of an unprepared heir); addiction and behavioral provisions (SOP 46 step 34).
8. **Write the one-page summary and communication plan (T21)** for every structure before it is signed — who will be told what, in what tone, by whom, when — and run the advance-notice rule (SOP 42).

### B. Ten-year trustscape review

9. **Run the review worksheet (T24 Part 2)** for the whole system: who serves as trustee and protector, and who the primary beneficiaries are; which trusts are active and funded; what each pays out now and is projected to pay over ten years; which will terminate within ten years; total fees to trustees, investment advisers, and other parties; what succession looks like if a trustee dies or steps down; what happens if a beneficiary or a whole class dies; where the foreseeable problem spots are.
10. **Tally the cost of the system** across trustee, adviser, custody, tax, and legal fees. A quarter or half a point of trustee fee stacked on management fees compounds over generations; say so in numbers.
11. **Report to the principals and the council** with recommendations: keep, simplify, modify, or commission a Family Trust Review.

### C. Family Trust Review

12. **Commission it on a trigger:** a generational transition; educating trustees, beneficiaries, or family-office and trust-company managers; a coming change in family or trust leadership; a termination or large principal distribution ahead; a decanting or reformation of a complex, costly system; the creation of new trusts or a private trust company. The perpetual-trust counterweight conversation belongs here too: before any dynasty structure, the family hears the unintended consequences (a class of beneficiaries who never own anything), society's likely view, and the certainty that nothing lasts forever.
13. **Engage the reviewer.** Usually an EEN consultant experienced in multigenerational family enterprise; the Advisor coordinates and supplies data. Scope, timeline, interviewees, and fee on T24 Part 1.
14. **Three research activities:** interview family leaders (and, where wise, adult beneficiaries and trustees); study written and oral accounts of the family's and the enterprise's history; review existing structures and documents.
15. **Five deliverables:** a summary of family history with short biographies of leaders past and present; a history of the family enterprise and its philanthropy; a summary of the family's prominent values; a description of the existing trust system — architecture, how the trusts interact, and their costs; recommendations for trust-system design and for family education.
16. **Present to the family** at a family meeting (SOP 43); feed recommendations into the advanced-planning tracker (T10), the governance work (SOP 45), and the learning plans (T19).

### D. Modify, succeed, and guard

17. **Modification routes — involve every stakeholder.** Options counsel will weigh: a non-judicial or judicial settlement agreement (least burdensome; can shift income terms or settle an interpretation; generally cannot create or destroy beneficial interests); judicial modification or reformation (long, costly, no guarantee); decanting (a new trust and usually a new situs; can add a protector, change administration, divide trustee duties; generally not beneficial interests). Whatever the route, someone left out of even a beneficial change may be aggrieved years later.
18. **Trustee succession is a duty from day one.** Every trustee: keeps records a successor could pick up quickly; documents the reasoning behind major administration, investment, and distribution decisions; treats the beneficiary as a partner so skills outlive any one trustee; keeps candidates in mind and makes sure the beneficiary knows who holds the appointment power and has met the likely successors; and, where possible, serves side by side with the successor before stepping back. Where the beneficiary holds the power, the trustee guides them through interviewing and selecting.
19. **Trustee reaffirmation every five years.** Beneficiaries confirm, in good faith, that they wish the trustee to continue; a trustee not endorsed resigns. Where an instrument cannot be amended, sitting trustees may agree voluntarily; make acceptance of reaffirmation a condition for successor trustees.
20. **Peer review every three to five years** of each significant trust and foundation: reviewers of proven experience, independent of the family and its advisers, serving once, paid, one day on site; scope — fidelity to purpose, the empowerment of beneficiaries, beneficiary literacy and the mechanisms sustaining it, the trustee's awareness of each beneficiary's chosen path, and the policies and providers for investment, tax, and legal matters; conduct constructive; serious deficiencies stop the review and go to the selector; reviewers raise issues and **never** implement them; implementation is never assigned to a reviewer. The family council schedules it.
21. **Keep the conflicts register.** Acknowledge every structural conflict (drafting attorney as trustee; institutional trustee with in-house investment management; a [Firm] sister company serving as trustee while the Advisor advises the beneficiary; the Advisor's own fees), document it, disclose it to creator, trustees, protector, and adult beneficiaries, and record how it is managed. Review annually with [Firm Compliance].
22. **Assess a private trust company (PTC) when wealth approaches the threshold.** Factors: personnel turnover at commercial trustees; confidentiality; financial standing and investment competence; situs tax; insurance and bonding costs; the need for successors to individuals; chartering and operating cost; outside-trustee fees; the cost of managing in-house staff versus an outside relationship — and the subjective test: does a PTC fit the family's governance intentions? If yes, six practices: an annual or biennial meeting of beneficiaries, managers, and the owner-trustee; the family's values and history captured in a usable form for the independent managers; attention to the owner-trustee role; family engagement in the PTC's mission at least once a generation; the PTC as the locus of family education; the OTB available to every beneficiary. A protector remains necessary even with a family PTC.

### E. Close a trust well

23. **Start two years out.** Treat a scheduled termination or large principal distribution as the moment the trustee's highest duty stops being hypothetical: commission a review (Section C) and an OTB for each receiving beneficiary.
24. **Recognize a new gift.** If terminating assets flow into new trusts — by will, by the beneficiaries' own plans, or by choice — a new gift is being made: run the creator's questions (purpose, hopes, recipients, integration, joy) and write a new purpose statement.
25. **Terminate with care.** Full final accounting to present beneficiaries; a release after review; a closing gathering where beneficiaries reflect on the trust's effect, thank the creator and the trustees past and present, and discuss where they go from here; optionally a short written history of the trust for the family archive.
26. **Mark departures.** When a trustee, protector, or adviser leaves the trustscape: a dinner, a letter of appreciation, a passing of the torch — step back, reflect and give thanks, return carrying the learning.

## Follow-up

- Within one week of any design meeting: counsel briefed in writing; T21 summary drafted; decisions logged.
- Within 30 days of a ten-year review: report delivered; tracker (T10) updated with actions coded as completed / in progress / future.
- Family Trust Review: recommendations tracked to closure at each annual family meeting.
- Annually: conflicts register reviewed; succession answers refreshed; reaffirmation and peer-review dates checked on the calendar (T15).

## Do / Don't

**Do**

- Choose the protector before the trustee.
- Put every conflict on paper and in front of every adult party.
- Start termination work two years early.
- Ask what harm a structure could do before asking what good.

**Don't**

- Don't let the DC or the protector decide a distribution.
- Don't fold the Office of the Beneficiary into the family office.
- Don't sell perpetuity as a tax product; put the counterweight in front of the client.
- Don't leave any beneficiary class out of a modification, however beneficial.

## Mode A / Mode B

### Mode A — whole family

- The Advisor coordinates the whole system with the family's counsel and trustees; the trust map on T14 is the master inventory.
- The family council receives the ten-year review and schedules peer reviews and reaffirmations.

### Mode B — individual within a larger team

- The family's trust system is theirs; the Advisor contributes to reviews and designs only when invited by the FCT or a trustee.
- For his own individuals, he prepares them for terminations and successions, helps them convene an OTB, and advises on requests (SOP 46) within the mandate on T05.
- Where [Trust Co.] is the family's trustee, the confidentiality wall inside [Firm] applies to everything the Advisor learns from his individual.

## Metrics

- Trusts with a named protector and a functioning distribution-advice mechanism as a share of significant trusts.
- Ten-year review refreshed within the last three years (yes/no per family).
- Conflicts register reviewed and disclosed in the last twelve months (yes/no).
